TCP Reliability and Congestion Control

TCP provides a reliable, ordered byte stream over an unreliable packet
network. It achieves this with sequence numbers, cumulative acknowledgements,
checksums, and retransmission on timeout or duplicate acknowledgement.

The three-way handshake establishes a connection. The client sends a SYN with
an initial sequence number, the server replies SYN-ACK with its own, and the
client responds ACK. Both sides now know each other's starting sequence
numbers. Connection teardown uses a four-way exchange because each direction
closes independently; a host that has finished sending may still receive.

Flow control and congestion control solve different problems and are often
confused. Flow control stops a fast sender from overwhelming a slow receiver,
and is handled by the receiver advertising a window. Congestion control stops
senders collectively from overwhelming the network itself, and is inferred
rather than advertised, because routers do not tell endpoints they are
congested. TCP treats packet loss as the congestion signal.

Slow start begins with a small congestion window and doubles it every round
trip, which is exponential growth despite the name. On reaching the slow
start threshold it switches to congestion avoidance, growing by roughly one
segment per round trip. On three duplicate acknowledgements it halves the
window and continues, called fast recovery. On a timeout, which indicates
more severe loss, it drops back to the initial window and restarts slow
start.

The reason loss is used as the signal is historical and increasingly
awkward. On wireless links, packets are lost to interference rather than
congestion, so TCP needlessly slows down. On deeply buffered links, routers
absorb excess packets instead of dropping them, so loss arrives far too
late and latency balloons. This is bufferbloat. Newer algorithms such as BBR
model the bottleneck bandwidth and round-trip time directly rather than
waiting for loss.
