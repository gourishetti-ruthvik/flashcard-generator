# The Laws of Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics describes how energy moves and transforms, and what those
transformations cannot do. Its laws are unusual among physical laws in being
statements of impossibility: they say what no machine, however cleverly
built, will ever achieve. The framework was assembled in the nineteenth
century largely by engineers trying to make steam engines less wasteful, and
only afterwards understood as deep statements about the universe.

The zeroth law states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium
with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other. It sounds trivial and
was numbered zero because it was recognised as necessary only after the other
three were already named. Its content is real: it is what makes temperature a
meaningful, measurable quantity at all. Without transitivity of equilibrium,
a thermometer would tell you about the thermometer, not about the thing being
measured.

The first law is conservation of energy applied to thermal systems. The
change in a system's internal energy equals the heat added to it minus the
work it does on its surroundings. Energy is never created or destroyed, only
moved and converted. The practical consequence is that a perpetual motion
machine of the first kind, one producing work from nothing, is impossible.
Every claimed example has turned out to have an unnoticed energy input.

The second law is the one with teeth. In an isolated system, entropy never
decreases. Equivalently, heat does not flow spontaneously from a colder body
to a hotter one, and no cyclic process converts heat entirely into work. This
rules out a perpetual motion machine of the second kind: one that would
extract heat from the ocean and drive a ship with it, violating nothing about
energy conservation while still being impossible. The Carnot efficiency,
one minus the ratio of cold to hot absolute temperatures, sets a ceiling on
any heat engine operating between two reservoirs, and no engineering
improvement can exceed it.

Entropy is often glossed as disorder, which is a poor summary. It is better
understood as a count: the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with
what is observed macroscopically. A gas fills its container not because it
prefers to, but because overwhelmingly more arrangements have it spread out
than clustered in one corner. The second law is therefore statistical rather
than absolute. A gas spontaneously gathering in a corner is not forbidden,
merely so improbable that it will not be seen in the lifetime of the
universe. This distinguishes the second law from the first, which admits no
exceptions of any probability.

The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as
temperature approaches absolute zero. Its practical consequence is that
absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps: each stage of
cooling extracts less than the last, and the target recedes. Temperatures
within a billionth of a degree of absolute zero have been achieved in
laboratories, and the remaining gap is not a matter of better engineering.

Taken together the laws have been summarised, cynically and accurately, as:
you cannot win, you cannot break even, and you cannot leave the game.
